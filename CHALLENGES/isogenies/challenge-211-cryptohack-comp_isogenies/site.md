## Statement (from remaining_descs.json)

You may have noticed two things so far. One is that the algorithms you're using are linear in the degree of the isogeny (for a degree n-isogeny generated by a point of order n, we need to compute all n multiples of the kernel. The second is that the shape of the prime tells you all rational points will have order dividing $2^{18} \cdot 3^{13}$, which have smooth order. It turns out that for composite degree isogenies, we can compute an isogeny of order $n = p_0^{e_0} \cdot p_1^{e_1} \cdot \ldots \cdot p_k^{e_k}$, by chaining $e_0$ isogenies of degree $p_0$, followed by $e_1$ isogenies of degree $p_1$ and so on. This is significantly faster than enumerating all $n$ multiples of the kernel generator!! For an isogeny $\phi : E \to E'$, we know evaluating a point in the kernel through this isogeny will give us the identity point. If we take the kernel generator $K$ and scale it to have order $p_0$, then we can compute the isogeny $\phi_0$ of degree $p_0$ and if we evaluate the kernel generator $K' = \phi_0(K)$ we will have a point of order $p_0^{e_0 - 1} \cdot p_1^{e_1} \cdot \ldots \cdot p_k^{e_k}$. We can do this for every prime one by one until we have computed the entire isogeny chain. For this challenge, your goal is to compute the $3^{13}$-isogeny with domain $E$ and kernel generated by $K$: $E : y^{2} = x^{3} + x, \quad K = (357834818388i + 53943911829, 46334220304i + 267017462655), \quad p = 2^{18} \cdot 3^{13} - 1$
